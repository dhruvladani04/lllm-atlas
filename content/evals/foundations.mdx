Two disciplines share the word "evaluation" and almost nothing else.

**Model evaluation** asks whether one foundation model is better than another. It runs a
fixed set of questions against many models and produces a number you can rank on. That is
what sections 1 and 2 of this site are: scores, and the health of the benchmarks behind
them.

**Application evaluation** asks whether _your_ system works. It runs your inputs through
your prompts, your retrieval, your tools and your model, and asks whether the result was
any good. RAGAS and DeepEval will not tell you whether Claude beats Gemini. They tell you
whether your pipeline hallucinates.

Confusing the two is the most common mistake in this area, and it is expensive in both
directions. Teams pick a model from a leaderboard and never measure their own system.
Or they build an elaborate eval harness for their application and use it to argue about
which model is smarter, which it cannot answer.

## The unit of evaluation

Here is the idea the rest of this section hangs on: **the thing you score is not always a
response.** Most eval tooling assumes one input, one output, one score, because that is the
shape of a benchmark question. Real applications are not that shape.

| Archetype             | What you must score                                                                  |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Plain generation      | One response                                                                         |
| RAG                   | One response **plus its retrieved context** — two components that fail independently |
| Agentic               | **The trajectory** — the whole sequence of tool calls                                |
| Multi-turn            | **The session** — memory, contradiction, degradation across turns                    |
| Structured extraction | **Each field**, not the object                                                       |

Get the unit wrong and your numbers are confidently meaningless. Score a RAG system at the
response level and you cannot tell a retrieval miss from a model that ignored good context —
two failures with opposite fixes. Score an agent at the response level and a run that
reached the right answer through six wrong tool calls looks identical to one that went
straight there.

<Callout type="pitfall">
  A single number that goes up is not evidence that your system improved. It is evidence
  that the thing you chose to measure went up. Those are different claims, and the gap
  between them is where most eval work goes wrong.
</Callout>

## Why one pipeline is never enough

An eval pipeline encodes one definition of "good". Applications have several, and they
conflict:

- **Correctness** — is the answer right?
- **Groundedness** — is it supported by the context you retrieved?
- **Format** — will the downstream parser accept it?
- **Safety** — does it leak, refuse badly, or take the bait on injection?
- **Cost and latency** — could you afford to serve it?

A change that improves one usually costs another. Longer, more careful answers score better
on correctness and worse on latency and cost. Stricter grounding reduces hallucination and
increases refusals on questions your corpus almost covers. You need separate pipelines
because you need to see the trade, not a blended number that hides it.

## Reference-based and reference-free

**Reference-based** evaluation compares output to a known-good answer. It is precise, it is
cheap to run, and it requires someone to write the answers. It works for extraction,
classification and anything with a correct result.

**Reference-free** evaluation judges output on its own terms — usually with another model
as the judge — against a rubric. It scales to open-ended tasks where no single answer is
correct, and it inherits every bias of the judge.

That inheritance is not hypothetical. Several benchmarks on this site are themselves scored
by a model:

The second one matters more than the first. MT-Bench is nearing saturation, so its judge
is a historical curiosity. Humanity's Last Exam is _active_ — a benchmark people quote
today — and its answers are checked by a model. Every score on it inherits that checker's
failure modes.

When the judge is a model, you are measuring agreement with that model's taste. That is
often good enough. It is never the same as correctness, and a rubric that a judge scores
highly can still be a rubric your users hate.

<Callout type="note">
  Use reference-based evaluation wherever you can write the reference. Reach for a judge
  when you genuinely cannot — then validate the judge against human labels on a sample,
  and re-validate it whenever you change the judge model.
</Callout>

## Offline and online

**Offline** evals run against a fixed set before you ship: a golden set, in CI, gating a
deploy. They are repeatable and they catch regressions.

**Online** evals run against live traffic after you ship: sampling real sessions, scoring
them, watching signals like task completion, user corrections and retries. They catch what
your golden set never contained — which is most of what actually breaks.

You need both, and they are not the same measurement. A golden set is a fixed distribution
you chose; production is a moving one your users chose. A system can hold steady offline
while degrading online simply because the traffic changed.
